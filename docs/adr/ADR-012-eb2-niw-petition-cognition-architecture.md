# ADR-012: EB-2 NIW Petition Cognition Architecture

**Status:** Accepted
**Date:** 2026-05-31
**Authors:** Architect (Claude)
**Supersedes:** None
**Related:** ADR-004 (Provenance-first legal AI), ADR-010 (KB operationalization), ADR-011 (Human review semantics)

---

## Context

Through Sprints 4–7.7, the platform evolved from a six-section flat generation model into a hierarchical petition cognition architecture. The original six sections — background, experience, expert_opinion, achievements, impact, conclusion — were designed to cover the right legal topics but compressed what a real EB-2 NIW petition expands into 13,000–15,000 words across a structured three-prong legal argument.

User 0 operational testing with real Felipe Cusnir case documents revealed the gap. A single expert opinion letter from Brian Leonardi ran 19 pages and covered all three Dhanasar prongs with dedicated subsections for academic record, certifications, role-by-role experience, professional memberships, peer recognition, national importance argument, and labor market waiver argument. The platform was collapsing all of that into one section.

Review of two real EB-2 NIW petition letters (Thalia Kreling Torezan, Dr. Thelia Dias de Castro) confirmed the structural pattern. A real petition letter runs 13,000–15,000 words and follows the Dhanasar prong hierarchy explicitly. Every section knows which prong it serves. The document reads top to bottom as a structured legal argument, not a collection of topics.

---

## Problem Statement

Three problems with the original six-section architecture:

**Problem 1 — Prong conflation.** Prong 1 (substantial merit and national importance) and Prong 3 (benefit of waiving labor certification) were both compressed into a single "impact" section. A real petition devotes 2,500–3,500 words to Prong 1 alone across four subsections, and 2,000–2,800 words to Prong 3 across five subsections.

**Problem 2 — Expert opinion compression.** A single "expert_opinion" section collapsed what should be one dedicated section per expert letter. Each expert contributes distinct authority, distinct evidentiary emphasis, and distinct legal weight for specific Dhanasar prongs. One expert letter = one cognition unit = one petition section.

**Problem 3 — Missing legal structure.** The original architecture had no introduction, no statement of law, no advanced degree qualification section, no role-by-role experience structure, no lectures and publications section, no peer recognition section separate from expert opinion, and no dedicated labor market shortage or economic benefit argument.

---

## Decision

The platform adopts a hierarchical petition cognition architecture organized around the Dhanasar prong structure. Every section knows its prong. Expert opinion sections are generated dynamically — one per expert letter. The document reads as a structured legal argument from top to bottom.

---

## The Dhanasar Framework

All EB-2 NIW petitions are evaluated under Matter of Dhanasar, 26 I&N Dec. 884 (AAO 2016). A National Interest Waiver is granted when the petitioner satisfies all three prongs by a preponderance of the evidence:

- **Prong 1:** The proposed endeavor has both substantial merit and national importance
- **Prong 2:** The petitioner is well-positioned to advance the proposed endeavor
- **Prong 3:** On balance, it would be beneficial to the United States to waive the job offer and labor certification requirements

The petition architecture must reflect this prong structure explicitly. Every generated section serves a named prong or a structural role. No section is prong-agnostic.

## Evidence Type Architecture

Each section consumes one or both of two evidence types:

**Petitioner evidence** — uploaded case documents: CV, expert opinion letters, support letters, certifications, professional records, professional plan. Fully provenance-traced in `generation_traces`. Every claim is citation-backed to a specific source document.

**External source intelligence** — industry publications, policy reports, federal agency data, market studies. Uploaded by the attorney as KB documents. Retrieved via KB RAG pipeline. Stored in `kb_guidance_traces`, structurally separate from `generation_traces`.

The provenance separation invariant applies to all sections. External source intelligence never enters `generation_traces`. An attorney reading a generation trace sees only petitioner evidence — never institutional style or external policy context presented as case evidence.

---

## Dynamic Expert Opinion Architecture

Expert opinion sections are the only dynamically generated section type.

**Section code pattern:** `prong2_expert_opinion_{expert_name_slug}`

Example: Brian Leonardi → `prong2_expert_opinion_brian_leonardi`

**Generation pipeline behavior:**
1. Chunks from expert opinion letters are classified with `section_affinity = prong2_expert_opinion_base`
2. The expert's name is extracted from the document metadata or classification
3. At generation time, the pipeline detects expert opinion documents, creates a dynamic section code per expert, clones the `prong2_expert_opinion_base` template, substitutes `{{expert_name}}` with the actual expert's full name and title, and generates one section per expert
4. The dynamic section code is persisted on `draft_sections.section_code`
5. The UI renders expert opinion sections under Prong 2 with the expert's full name and title as the section header

## Coverage Gate Semantics

**Blocking sections** — generation halts if no evidence and `force_generate=False`:
- `prong1_endeavor_description`
- `prong2_educational_background`
- `prong2_professional_experience`
- `petition_conclusion`

**Sentinel sections** — return `INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE` sentinel if no classified evidence; LLM is never called:
- All other Prong 2 sections
- All Prong 3 sections
- All expert opinion sections — if no expert opinion evidence exists, sentinel is returned; hallucination is never permitted

**Structural sections** — always generated; evidence is optional:
- `introduction`
- `statement_of_law`

---

## Backward Compatibility

The original six section codes remain in `section_affinity_reference` and `prompt_templates`. Existing `draft_sections`, `generation_traces`, and `classification_results` referencing old codes are never modified. Prior drafts remain fully retrievable and reviewable. New cases use the new section taxonomy.

---

## Consequences

**Positive:**
- Petition structure matches real legal practice — 11,000–15,000 words across a full Dhanasar argument
- Each prong is argued with appropriate depth and dedicated sections
- Expert opinion sections are individually traceable, reviewable, and named
- External source intelligence is structurally separated from petitioner evidence
- Coverage gate is meaningful — each section has clear evidence requirements and sentinel behavior
- Attorney review is organized by prong, matching how attorneys think about these petitions

**Negative:**
- Variable section count per case increases generation pipeline complexity
- Dynamic expert opinion section codes require runtime slug generation
- Classification routing table requires updating for new section codes
- UI must render prong groupings dynamically
- Existing QA case drafts use old section taxonomy — new drafts will have a different structure

---

**This ADR governs all future prompt template additions, section taxonomy changes, classification routing decisions, and coverage gate rules for EB-2 NIW petitions. Any addition of a new section must specify its prong assignment, evidence type, word count target, and sentinel behavior before implementation begins.**
